Executive Summary

  • Horizon Kinetics argues the 2010s — sub-2% inflation, near-zero rates, roughly 2% real growth — were a historical anomaly sustained by one-off tailwinds that are now reversing. A structurally higher and more volatile inflation baseline of 3–5% is the base case for the next decade.
  • The firm's edge is a genuine three-to-five year time horizon, independent thinking, and a contrarian streak inherited from founders Murray Stahl and Peter Doyle, who left Bankers Trust over a rejection of Modern Portfolio Theory in favour of Buffett/Munger-style value investing.
  • The preferred vehicle is capital-light ownership of scarce real assets — royalties, exchanges, land leases — where inflation upside is captured without bearing the capex, working capital, or operating leverage of the underlying commodity business.
  • Right-tail optionality is the core valuation insight: these businesses carry embedded upside — brownfield expansion accruing to royalties for free, non-producing assets gaining value as discovery gets harder — that the market under-prices relative to yield-driven hard-asset businesses.
  • Capital allocation discipline and management quality are the primary risk factors. Forced growth, pro-cyclical M&A, and leverage are the classic ways these stories fail.

Investment Thesis

The thesis rests on a structural, not cyclical, inflation call. Fiscal dominance, a labour force that is neutral at best, a productivity ceiling even under an optimistic AI scenario, and fifteen years of underinvestment in physical supply chains collide with an AI-driven step-change in power and materials demand. If that holds, the businesses best positioned are not the miners and infrastructure owners themselves but the capital-light layers sitting on top of them — royalties, exchanges, and land leases — which capture the inflation and volume upside without carrying the balance-sheet risk. The market, in Davolos's framing, prices these more like yield instruments than like call options, which is where the mispricing sits.

Research

1. The Firm: Horizon Kinetics

Founded by Murray Stahl (special-situations equity at Bankers Trust through the 1980s and 90s), Peter Doyle, and Steven Bregman, the firm's founders left when a new CIO pushed Efficient Market Hypothesis and Modern Portfolio Theory — an approach they viewed as incompatible with their Buffett/Munger training. The name "Horizon" encodes the thesis directly: the only edge against better-resourced Park Avenue rivals is a genuinely long time horizon, thinking in three-to-five year terms without deviating for compensation cycles. The firm remains founder-led, with a culture oriented around independent, ideological investing rather than AUM growth or prestige. Stahl passed away shortly before this episode was recorded; the hosts note his legacy as an original, independent thinker who invested over time horizons far longer than most.

2. Davolos's Career Arc

Davolos joined the firm in December 2005 on the trading desk, entering orders for Murray Stahl's hedge funds, mutual funds, and managed accounts — an unglamorous foot in the door. Mentor Peter Doyle recognised his ambition and invited him into Murray's research meetings, where he understood perhaps a tenth of the discussion at first but recognised he was around unusually original thinkers. The firm sponsored his night-school MBA at NYU rather than sending him away for two years full-time. He entered during the 2005–07 commodity boom, when the funds ranked top percentile, then lived through the 2008 crash — a formative experience compounded by his parents having lost nearly all of their net worth in the S&L crisis. He has since risen to co-portfolio manager of the Inflation Beneficiaries ETF (INFL), the Internet Fund, and several private funds, across roughly twenty years at one firm.

3. Structural Inflation Thesis

Why the 2010s were anomalous: post-GFC overcapacity that took years to absorb; BRICS economies absorbing global supply; the shale revolution suppressing oil prices; the opening of Central Asian and post-Soviet mining assets; internet productivity gains still being accrued; and globalisation mobilising emerging-market labour.

Why 3–5% secular inflation is the base case going forward:

  • Fiscal dominance — the US is running structural 6–7% deficits (roughly $2 trillion), levels that would once have signalled crisis.
  • Labour is neutral at best — ageing Western populations and restricted US immigration limit the labour-supply offset.
  • A productivity ceiling — even if AI mirrors the internet's historical productivity impact (roughly 3% annualised over a decade), that only supports about 3% real growth, not enough to outgrow the debt load without inflation filling the gap.
  • Recursive fiscal feedback — government spending is itself a major driver of GDP, both directly through employment and indirectly through the end markets it supports.
  • A supply/demand collision — fifteen years of underinvestment in copper, iron, and hydrocarbons left supply chains sized for mature industries just as AI demands power-grid growth the system hasn't delivered in twenty years. Emerging markets, roughly 80% of global population, converging toward Western consumption levels compounds the constraint.

Davolos distinguishes broad inflation — noisy, sensitive to CPI quirks like owners' equivalent rent and hedonic adjustments — from localised inflation, which is already visible in parabolic moves across data-centre-adjacent names: EPC firms, copper wiring, turbine manufacturers.

4. Capital-Light Real Assets — The Core Framework

The framework grew out of a post-GFC exercise: which businesses survived, and why? The problem with scarce hard-asset businesses is that they're often great assets but poor businesses — heavy capex, working capital intensity, and operating leverage. The solution is to overlay a capital-light business model that captures the inflation upside of the underlying asset without taking on its balance-sheet risk.

5. Right-Tail Optionality — The Valuation Insight

Davolos pushes back on the fashionable "HALO" framing — High Asset, Low Obsolescence. A HALO business (a copper miner, a toll road, an apartment building) has great assets and attractive cash flows, but low optionality: expanding revenue requires heavy capex at modest returns on assets, producing a yield-driven profile. Capital-light businesses are structurally different — exchanges can turn volume over near-infinitely, and a miner's brownfield expansion accrues to the royalty holder for free. Franco-Nevada and Wheaton hold hundreds of non-producing assets the market treats as worthless, but as commodity prices rise and new discoveries become scarcer and costlier, that option value rises with them. The resulting profile is asymmetric and compounding, not simply a yield: underwrite a conservative base case from known reserves and run-rate volumes, then treat the increasing likelihood of the right tail as upside rather than as part of the required return.

6. Case Study: Landbridge

Landbridge is a triple-net industrial land lease and land royalty business in West Texas, part of FivePoint Infrastructure, with sister company Waterbridge building the physical pipes and wells. It earns roughly 90% margin on water-infrastructure revenue. The mechanics: for every barrel of oil or gas produced from the Delaware basin (the western Permian), roughly four barrels of wastewater must be transported, remediated, and disposed of — and once a well is drilled, that water flow continues for decades alongside the oil and gas, making the revenue recurring rather than cyclical. Under the general-partner structure, Waterbridge bears all the capex and opex while Landbridge, the land lessor, collects the royalty — upside without capital deployment. Even if Permian production overall peaks, growth in the Delaware basin can offset declines in the Midland basin, and producers running out of pore space (the rock porosity needed for storage) allows Landbridge to gain share at higher rates on long-term contracted barrels. Data centres now being developed in West Texas add a further, long-tail water-demand overlay as a free call option.

7. Case Study: Miami International Holdings

Miami International is a stock exchange Horizon Kinetics helped assemble decades ago, which listed publicly in 2025. Its throughput is mostly multi-listed options — options on everyday securities, with a low barrier to entry and lower margin but high growth. Pre-pandemic daily volume was around 20 million contracts; it now runs near 70 million. Incumbents such as ICE and CBOE largely ceded this space because competing for share in multi-listed options would have diluted the margins of their VIX and derivatives franchises, leaving Miami with roughly 20% market share. Cash flow from the lower-margin base funds new higher-margin products, such as Bloomberg 500 index futures and multi-leg options, and the underlying volume drivers — nominal growth and volatility — are themselves inflation-correlated: more nominal growth means more transactional volume, and more volatility means more hedging activity and wider spreads. Product innovation is the further right-tail option the market doesn't yet price in.

8. Competitive Dynamics in Royalties

The best time to be a royalty company is a brutal bear market — Franco-Nevada and Wheaton added once-in-a-generation assets between 2010 and 2014, when gold and bulk metals were obliterated. Today's environment is different: capital markets are not throwing money at precious-metal miners despite higher prices, which favours large royalty providers with balance sheets and expertise. Hybrid structures have emerged as a result — Franco does convertible preferred plus equity in exchange for royalties, functioning as an incubator portfolio, while Wheaton structured a large silver byproduct stream with BHP on a mine where it already owned a third of the royalty, combining information asymmetry with being the only checkbook in the room. On the land side, Landbridge's roughly 400,000 acres, smaller than Texas Pacific's roughly one million, are worth more per acre within its integrated Waterbridge, data-centre, and fibre-optic ecosystem than disparate acreage held by independents — a value that no other holder can currently justify paying for.

9. Risks and Red Flags

RiskDescription
Forced growthThe number one royalty mistake — overpaying in M&A, entering unfamiliar jurisdictions, or using leverage to force deals through.
Pro-cyclical reserve chasingManagement teams chasing the cycle higher instead of exercising prudence in upcycles and opportunism in downcycles.
Jurisdictional shockFranco-Nevada lost roughly 20–25% of NAV when Panama closed the Cobre Panama mine — a premier company, in a strong jurisdiction, with a world-class asset, still hit from outside its models.
Idiosyncratic concentrationThese businesses concentrate by asset class, customer, geography, and commodity, so diversification across sub-industries is essential.

The stated mitigation is a diversified portfolio spanning precious-metal streamers, energy royalties, and exchanges — underwriting the base case conservatively and treating optionality strictly as upside, never as part of the required return.

10. Career and Mentorship Notes

Davolos frames personal decision-making around a long time horizon: focus on what will still matter in five, ten, fifteen, or twenty-five years, and filter out distractions that won't. On career, he argues hard work alone isn't sufficient — most highly successful people had a mentor in one of two capacities, either advising on career management or taking a genuine personal interest and actively pushing the career forward. He credits Peter Doyle with serving both roles across twenty years.

Transferable Mental Models

  1. Who bears the capex, who captures the royalty? A lens for evaluating any platform, marketplace, or toll-road business model, not just equities.
  2. Optionality pricing. Underwrite a conservative base case and treat embedded upside as a free option rather than as required return — applies equally to venture, career, and project decisions.
  3. Structural versus cyclical drivers. Build a thesis from irreversible structural shifts — demographics, supply underinvestment, fiscal dominance — rather than from forecasting noisy aggregates like CPI prints.
  4. Time horizon as a competitive advantage. The freedom to think in three-to-five year terms while others are forced to optimise for quarterly cycles.
